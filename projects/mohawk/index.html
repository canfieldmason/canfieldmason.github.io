Carpet Roll Length Optimization

Engineering Intern, Mohawk Industries — Glasgow, KY

Six Sigma Minitab 22 Regression ANOVA / Tukey HSD Process Capability I-MR Control Charts SQL (SEQUEL) Excel Energy Analysis Internship

Every roll of carpet coming off the tufting line at Glasgow was wound to the same preset — 900 yards — regardless of what was on it. A dense, thin-piled style and a heavy, lofty one got the same number, which meant one was leaving hundreds of yards of drum capacity unused while the other was running within an inch of the point where the roll has to be split. Over a summer I measured 226 rolls across 31 styles, built a geometric and statistical model of roll thickness, and turned "load it by feel" into capability-tested wind targets. A second project with the maintenance department applied the fan affinity laws to a supply fan on a VFD and produced a validated energy savings case alongside it.

Presented at the Mohawk intern expo at headquarters.

How the Project Started

I came into Glasgow as a mechanical engineering intern without this project assigned. It surfaced the way most useful problems do — as a complaint. Rolls were being split because they came out too big, and other rolls were going out light, and nobody could tell me which styles did which or why. The preset was 900 yards because 900 yards was what the preset had always been.

The legacy tool for this was a TDAS equation of the form 1190 × e−0.017 × FW, a single exponential in face weight. When I fit it against current production data it was clearly drifted — the coefficients had been set against a product mix that no longer existed. That gave the project its shape: not "tune the old equation," but "measure what is actually on the drum and build the model back up from geometry."

Photo: tufting line / finished rolls on the floor at Glasgow

The Problem: Loading By Feel

A roll is wound on a 4.25″ radius cardboard core and can grow to a maximum outer radius of about 21.5″ before it exceeds handling and racking limits. How many yards fit inside that annulus depends entirely on how thick one wrapped layer of that particular carpet is — which varies with face weight, stitch density, pile height, and backing. A flat yardage preset ignores all of it.

Two real examples from the floor made the case better than any statistic:

Style Wound to Avg. outer radius Headroom left Consequence
83103 900 yd 18.9″ 2.6″ Roughly 200 yards of capacity left on the drum, every roll
83160 900 yd 20.2″ 1.4″ Under 100 yards of room — same preset, nearly at the split limit
Why the two failure modes are not symmetric: under-loading quietly loses capacity at every downstream step — more rolls per order, more handling, more truck space, more rack positions. Over-loading is loud and expensive: the roll has to be split, which forces a seam, wastes yardage on both sides of the cut, and creates a quality event that can come back as a customer claim. A good target pushes toward the ceiling without ever touching it, which is precisely a process capability problem.

Data Collection

There was no instrumented way to get layer thickness, so I measured it by hand and backed it out of the geometry. For every roll: a tape measure from the outside of the core to the outer edge of the roll, taken at the 4 and 8 o'clock positions and averaged, to cancel the ovality a roll picks up sitting on its side.

Step 1

Measure Two Radii

Tape measure from the edge of the cardboard core to the outer edge of the roll, at the 4 and 8 o'clock positions.

Step 2

Average and Record

Roll number and averaged radius into Excel, then style, actual production length, face weight, stitch rate and backing pulled from SEQUEL.

Step 3

Compute Thickness

Back the per-layer thickness out of the annulus the wound carpet occupies and the known production length.

Thickness = [ (ravg/12 + 4.25/12)2 − (4.25/12)2 ] ÷ Length Per-layer thickness in inches. Production length is taken from the precoat production length column in SEQUEL, which is always populated — tag length frequently is not, and using it silently drops rolls from the dataset.

The K Constant and Inverse Thickness

Maximum length is a pure geometry problem once thickness is known. The wound carpet occupies the annulus between the core and the maximum radius, so the usable cross-sectional area divided by the layer thickness gives the length — the same way you would find the length of a rolled-up tape.

rMAX = 21.5″,   rcore = 4.25″
LMAX = π ( rMAX2 − rcore2 ) ÷ Thickness
π ( 21.52 − 4.252 ) = 1394.7 in2
1394.7 ÷ 36 ÷ (1/12) = 14.0717 = K
LMAX = 14.0717 ÷ Thickness K folds the drum geometry and the inch-to-yard conversion into one constant, so every downstream result is expressed in the same units the floor uses.

The statistically important move was to stop modeling thickness and start modeling its reciprocal. MaxLength is a hyperbolic function of thickness, which is awkward to regress, badly behaved at the low end, and produces non-constant residual variance. Inverting it fixes all three at once:

Inv_T = 1 / Thickness   ⟹   MaxLength = K × Inv_T High Inv_T means a thinner layer, and therefore a longer roll. Because the relationship to length is now strictly linear, every tool downstream — linear regression, ANOVA, control charts, capability indices — becomes valid on Inv_T and translates directly back into yards by multiplying by K.

Worked example — style 83103, 16 samples

  • Average measured thickness: 0.12592″
  • Inverse thickness: 79.4
  • MaxLength = 14.0717 × 79.4 = 1,117 yards theoretical
  • Capability tested at a 1,020 yard target: Ppk = 1.47

1,117 yards is where the roll physically runs out of drum. 1,020 is where it can be wound every time without one going over. The gap between those two numbers is the entire point of the capability analysis.

Sorting the Styles: ANOVA and Tukey HSD

With 226 samples across 31 styles, the first question was whether styles actually differ in Inv_T or whether the spread was just measurement noise — and if they do differ, which ones are genuinely distinct from each other rather than merely different on paper. That is a one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD, run in Minitab.

F = 39.54 One-way ANOVA on Inv_T by style
p < 0.001 Style differences are real, not noise
R² = 88.3% Variance in Inv_T explained by style identity
s = 2.54 Pooled standard deviation, 14 distinct Tukey groups

Tukey grouped the 31 styles into 14 statistically distinguishable sets. Fourteen presets is not something an operator is going to manage, so I collapsed the groups into six tiers that respect the Tukey boundaries but are coarse enough to actually run:

Tier Inv_T range Theoretical max Styles
Tier 4 78.9 – 81.6 ~1,100 – 1,150 yd 80664, 80669, 80678, 80665, 83155, 83103, 80519, 88922
Tier 3H 75.7 – 79.9 ~1,065 – 1,125 yd 80543, 80561, 80557, 80668, 88965, 83172
Tier 3 71.5 – 75.4 ~1,007 – 1,061 yd 88923, 80743, 83152, 83145, 83160
Tier 2 63.9 – 70.1 ~900 – 987 yd 8M063, 83144, 80528, 88998, 83183, 80681
Tier 2B 64.5 (SD 5.25) ~908 yd 80558 — isolated for high variability
Tier 1 52.6 ~740 yd 81846 — shear pile

Two of those tiers exist because of variance rather than mean. 80558 sits squarely in the Tier 2 mean but carries a standard deviation of 5.25 against a pooled 2.54 — more than double the spread of its neighbours. Averaging it into the tier would have handed it a target it could not hold, so it got its own conservative bucket. 81846 is a shear pile construction whose behaviour has nothing to do with the loop styles around it.

A methodology trap worth naming: R² computed on per-style averages looks spectacular, because averaging has already removed the within-style scatter you are trying to model. The honest metric is roll-level R², computed against individual measurements. I kept both in front of me all summer specifically so I would not quote the flattering one.

Six Sigma: Control Charts and Process Capability

Knowing a style's average Inv_T tells you nothing about whether you can safely wind to it. Two styles with identical means and very different roll-to-roll spread need very different targets. This is the part of the project that made the results defensible rather than merely interesting, and it follows the standard order: prove stability first, then measure capability.

Step 1 — I-MR control charts

An Individuals and Moving Range chart confirms the winding process is in statistical control before its capability is assessed. Capability indices computed on an unstable process are meaningless — they describe a distribution that is not actually holding still. Points outside the control limits on either the individuals or the moving range chart mean the style gets reviewed rather than tiered. Every Tier 4 style came back in control.

Step 2 — Capability against a one-sided specification

This is a one-sided problem, which is what makes it interesting. There is no lower limit worth defending — a slightly short roll is a minor loss. There is only an upper limit: the radius at which the roll must be split. So the specification is set as an upper bound on Inv_T, derived from the target yardage through the same K constant:

USLInv_T = 14.0717 ÷ Target Yardage A candidate wind target converts directly into a specification limit on the measured quantity. Raising the target tightens the spec, and capability falls — which is exactly the tradeoff the analysis is meant to expose.
Index What it uses Reading
Cpk Within-subgroup (short-term) variation What the process is capable of at its best
Ppk Overall deviation, roll-to-roll and long-term What the process actually delivers in production
≥ 1.33 — Capable
≥ 1.67 — Six sigma capable

I reported Ppk as the governing number rather than Cpk. Cpk describes short-term capability and would have made several targets look better than they were; Ppk carries the between-run and between-order variation that a wind target has to survive in real production. Where the two diverged sharply, that gap was itself the finding: it means the style's sample was dominated by rolls from one or two production orders, and the between-order variation is being understated. Those styles were flagged as needing more diverse sampling before a target could be confirmed, not given a target anyway.

The Screening Regression

Tiers only help with styles I had measured. The plant runs far more styles than 31, and new ones arrive continuously. For those, I needed to predict Inv_T from specification data alone — numbers already sitting in TuftingSpecsPro before a single roll is wound.

Inv_T = 101.1 − 1.70(FW) − 0.0412(FW2) + 1.181(Stitches/in) FW in oz/yd². Stitches per inch is the stitch/3in specification divided by three. Multiply the result by K = 14.0717 for theoretical maximum yards.

Why these predictors

  • Face weight is the primary driver. More yarn per square yard means a taller pile and a thicker layer per wrap. It carries the bulk of the signal.
  • The squared face weight term captures diminishing returns. Thickness does not keep climbing linearly with face weight — at high weights the pile begins to support itself and compress differently under winding tension, and the curve flattens. A purely linear model systematically over-predicted thickness at the heavy end.
  • Stitches per inch was the term that separated styles the other predictors could not tell apart. A denser stitch pulls the pile tighter, so the same face weight compresses thinner per yard. This mattered directly: styles with identical face weight and identical specifications on paper were coming out hundreds of feet apart in capacity, and stitch density was the variable that explained it.

Worked example — style 83103 (FW = 14.0, stitch/3in = 30.6)

  • Inv_T = 101.1 − 1.70(14.0) − 0.0412(196) + 1.181(10.2) = 81.4
  • 81.4 × 14.0717 = 1,145 yd theoretical maximum
  • Lower 90% prediction bound: 1,053 yd

The measured Inv_T for this style, from 16 physical samples, was 79.4 — a theoretical 1,117 yards. The equation over-predicts by about 28 yards, which is the honest accuracy of a screening tool built on spec sheet columns.

What the equation cannot do

Roll-level R² sat at about 66%. That is genuinely useful for a first target on a style nobody has measured, and it is not good enough to set a confirmed wind target. I spent a fair amount of the summer establishing why, because a supervisor asking for a better number deserves an explanation rather than a shrug.

  • Specification columns describe what the style was designed to be. Two rolls built to identical specifications can behave differently on the drum because of loop geometry under winding tension — real texture that no spec column records.
  • Measured free-standing pile height correlates with face weight at r ≈ 0.97, VIF ≈ 17. It is effectively a restatement of face weight and adds almost nothing predictive, so it did not survive into the final form.
  • A Chow test on backing type returned p = 0.103 — a single equation legitimately fits both PF and woven backings when face weight is the predictor, so splitting the equation by backing was not statistically justified.
  • The ceiling on spec-sheet-only prediction for this dataset is roughly 40–50% of variance within standard stock. Physical caliper measurement per style is the only path past it, and would plausibly reach R² of 0.85–0.95. Communicating that ceiling clearly was as much of the deliverable as the equation itself.
Where the model is deliberately conservative: confirmed big-roll styles such as 83128 and 80561 out-perform the general equation by roughly 85 feet, because at high footage the pile compresses under its own accumulated winding tension in a way the model does not capture. I left that conservatism in rather than fitting around it. An equation that occasionally leaves 85 feet on the drum is a much cheaper error than one that occasionally forces a split.

Model stability

Before handing the equation over I checked that it was not being held up by a handful of points. Removing the two highest Cook's distance styles (8M042, 8M044) moved the coefficients by less than 0.5% and shifted MaxLength predictions by ±4 yards. The fit is stable. I also ran a Box-Cox transformation and AIC/BIC comparison against the exponential form my supervisor initially preferred, so the choice of functional form was an empirical result rather than my opinion.

Second Project: VFD Energy Savings on a Spinning Supply Fan

Alongside the roll work I partnered with the maintenance department on an energy case for a supply fan in spinning. The fan ran at full speed continuously; the question was what reduced-speed operation on a variable frequency drive would actually be worth. That is a fan affinity laws problem, and the answer is dramatic enough that it is worth writing out.

Q2/Q1 = (N2/N1)     ΔP2/ΔP1 = (N2/N1)2     P2/P1 = (N2/N1)3 Flow scales with speed, pressure with the square of speed, and shaft power with the cube. Airflow and power do not trade off one for one — this is what makes speed control on a fan so much better than throttling it.
Speed Airflow Shaft power Power reduction
100%100%100%—
90%90%72.9%27.1%
80%80%51.2%48.8%
70%70%34.3%65.7%

Giving up 20% of airflow buys back nearly half the power draw. The work was to establish what the process actually required rather than what the fan happened to be delivering, convert the resulting speed reduction into annual kilowatt-hours across real run hours, and validate the case against measured draw rather than nameplate figures.

What Actually Went to the Floor

A model nobody uses is a model that failed. The deliverable was not the regression — it was a decision rule simple enough to apply at a winder, backed by an Excel workbook that did the lookups.

Priority 1

Confirmed Capability Target

If the style has a capability-tested target with Ppk ≥ 1.33, use it. This is the strongest evidence available and overrides everything below.

Priority 2

Tier Assignment

If the style is measured but not yet capability confirmed, use its ANOVA/Tukey tier target.

Priority 3

Screening Prediction

No samples: run the regression on spec sheet data and use the lower 90% bound as a starting target.

Fallback

Analog Method

For a brand new style with unusual specs, match to the nearest spec-similar known style and inherit its target until samples exist.

The workbook behind it was built for the floor, not for me: AVERAGEIF across sheets, LARGE with tie-breaker helper columns, IFERROR wrapping on every operator-facing cell, and TEXT() wrapping on style code lookups because the codes are stored as numbers in text-formatted cells and a naive VLOOKUP silently returns #N/A. Deliberately no XLOOKUP — the deployment target had to stay LibreOffice compatible.

What I'd Do Differently

I would have started physical caliper measurement in week two instead of week eight. The single most valuable thing I learned is that the spec sheet has a hard predictive ceiling — roughly 40–50% of variance within standard stock — and no amount of clever modeling gets past it. Direct thickness measurement on a sample per style plausibly reaches R² of 0.85–0.95. I spent real time exhausting the regression approaches before accepting that the constraint was the data, not the method.

I would also build the sampling plan around production orders rather than rolls. Several styles showed large Ppk/Cpk gaps purely because their sample was dominated by rolls from one or two orders, which understates between-run variation and inflates apparent capability. Ten rolls from ten different orders would have been worth more than thirty from three.

The 8M series is still unsolved. Individual variance within those styles is high enough that spec-based prediction is not reliable at any reasonable sample size, and physical measurement is the only honest path. Naming that clearly was more useful to the plant than publishing an equation with a confidence interval wide enough to be meaningless.
